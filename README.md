# Autobiography of Helene Schaarschmidt, born 1903 in Plauen, Germany

This repository serves the purpose of transcribing and translating Helene Martha Schaarschmidt's autobiography. She was born in 1903 in Plauen. Her book opens with a short look back at her childhood and youth and then becomes a diary, kept from the late 1920s until January 1959: through the Weimar years, the Nazi era, the Second World War and the early GDR.

## Background

I have received a photocopied version of "Mein Tagebuch" from my grandmother Margot (born 1935), Helene's daughter. I scanned the full book of 76 pages and slowly work on transcribing it first in German, and then translating it into English. Given the context of her life in war-ridden Germany in the first half of the 20th century, her biography has some historical value for anyone interested in reading a first-hand report on living as a common German citizen during that period.
This book is published under an open license (CC0 1.0).

## Synopsis

Helene Martha Penzel was born on 14 November 1903 in Plauen, the daughter of a plumber. The first pages look back briefly on her childhood and youth; from the late 1920s on she writes as things happen, often naming the very day ("Today, Sunday 20.8.33, I am sitting here alone at home"). She is an ordinary working woman, not a political observer. The large events of her time enter the diary as they reached her through work, family, radio and newspapers, at times in the words of the regime's own propaganda.

- **1914–1918:** Her father is called up in the first days of the war and serves in Russia; he is still away when she is confirmed in 1918.
- **1920s:** She works as a saleswoman, a milliner's trimmer, a pattern girl at Gebr. Lange, and a cashier at the Hermann Tietz department store and then at the Epa store. She takes dance lessons and goes to balls in 1920–21. In the inflation of 1923 a loaf of bread costs 5 million marks. She spends a summer holiday near Bayreuth in 1928.
- **1931–1934:** She meets Walter Schaarschmidt of Neuensalz at Easter 1931. On 1 April 1933 her store is boycotted and occupied by Nazis, and its Jewish employees are dismissed. A week before her wedding a cousin is beaten to death by his own comrades. She marries Walter on 17 March 1934; the next morning he falls seriously ill with pneumonia.
- **1935–1939:** Her daughter Margot is born in 1935 and her son Günter in 1937. Walter suffers from a chronic skin disease and goes from one cure to the next. The Sudeten crisis of 1938 brings the first blackouts, and in September 1939 the war begins. Her brother serves as a pioneer in France and later in Russia.
- **1940–1943:** Rationing, collections for the front, and the bombing of the western cities. At Christmas 1941 her six-year-old nephew is killed by a coal lorry. Walter can no longer work, and she delivers newspapers. At the end of August 1943 Walter dies at home after a week of illness, which she describes hour by hour.
- **1944–1945:** Air-raid alarms come almost daily. In March 1945 the family is bombed out. She lives through the heaviest raid on Plauen in a dugout her father built in the garden, then flees with the children to relatives in Raun until the end of the war. Their flat is looted, and hunger follows.
- **1946–1959:** Her father dies in 1946 and her mother in 1949. Margot trains in a women's clothing workshop. Günter becomes a fitter, studies at the Workers' and Peasants' Faculty in Leipzig and becomes an officer in the National People's Army. Both children marry in 1957 and each has a child in 1958. The last entry, dated 19 January 1959, ends: "I have done my duty; now they must make their own way through life."

## How to contribute

There are two main ways to contribute:

1. If you can read old German handwriting, you can download the <a href="Mein%20Tagebuch__Helene%20Martha%20Schaarschmidt.pdf">scanned PDF file</a>
 and proofread the transcription in the LaTeX file against it, especially the readings marked `(?)`. This is done per page, as indicated in the .tex document. Please write each sentence in a new line. That makes correcting and tracking easier in the future. The German text is an edited reading edition (modern spelling, grammar errors corrected, her voice kept); the rules are in [docs/adr/0001-reading-edition.md](docs/adr/0001-reading-edition.md).
2. If you read both languages, you can check the English translation against the German text. Corrections to readings belong in Deutsch.tex first; English.tex follows it.
